Why did Russia sell Alaska? In 1867 it was hard to defend, far from St Petersburg and next door to British Canada, so Russia sold it to the United States for $7.2 million: about two cents an acre. Today Alaska pumps $7 million worth of oil every few hours.

◀◀ DEEP DIVE · 3 MIN READ

Why Russia Sold Alaska to the United States in 1867

Russia sold Alaska because it was hard to defend and Russia feared losing it to Britain in a future war. In 1867 it agreed to sell the territory to the United States for $7.2 million, about two cents an acre. Today Alaska produces that much money in oil every few hours.

What was Alaska before 1867?

Before the sale, the land was a Russian colony known as Russian America. The name "Alaska" was chosen by the United States after the purchase.

It was enormous. Alaska has about 1,481,346 km² of land, roughly 2.7 times the size of metropolitan France. The area covered by the treaty was about 1,518,800 km², or some 375 million acres.

Why did Russia want to sell Alaska?

After its defeat in the Crimean War, Russia saw Alaska as difficult to defend. Historians point to one main concern: in a future war, British forces could take the territory easily from neighbouring Canada. In 1867 the land to the east, including the Yukon, was British territory.

According to the US Office of the Historian, selling to the United States was a way to offset the designs of Great Britain in the region. If Russia was going to lose Alaska, it preferred that the United States have it.

How did the deal happen?

The treaty was negotiated by US Secretary of State William Seward and the Russian minister in Washington, Eduard de Stoeckl. After talks that ran through the night, they signed it in Washington at 4 a.m. on March 30, 1867.

The US Senate ratified it on April 9, 1867, by a vote of 37 to 2. The formal handover took place in Sitka on October 18, 1867, when the Russian flag came down and the US flag went up.

How much did the United States pay?

The price was $7,200,000. Divided over roughly 375 million acres, that comes to about 1.9 cents an acre, or around two cents. Adjusted with the consumer price index, $7.2 million in 1867 is roughly $164 million in 2026 dollars.

Was the Alaska Purchase called Seward's Folly?

Yes, by some. Critics dubbed it Seward's Folly and Seward's Icebox, mocking the idea of paying for a frozen land. Those nicknames stuck, but they did not reflect the whole picture: most of the press at the time supported the purchase.

What happened after the sale?

First came gold. The first major strike in Alaska was at Anvil Creek, near Nome, on September 20, 1898, and the Nome gold rush ran from 1899 to 1909.

Then came oil. The discovery at Prudhoe Bay was announced on March 12, 1968. It is the largest oil field in the United States.

How much oil does Alaska produce today?

In 2026 Alaska has been producing around 413,000 to 428,000 barrels a day, and Alaska North Slope crude has traded at around $100 or more. At about 420,000 barrels and $100 a barrel, that is roughly $42 million a day, or about $7.2 million every four hours. Even at much lower prices, such as $55 a barrel, it would take only around seven and a half hours.

So who got the better deal? Russia sold a territory it believed it could not hold. The United States bought land whose value only became clear decades later.

Key takeaways

  • Russia sold Alaska in 1867 because it was hard to defend and Russia feared losing it to Britain.
  • The price was $7.2 million, about two cents an acre.
  • The treaty was signed on March 30, 1867, and Alaska was handed over in Sitka on October 18, 1867.
  • Critics called it Seward's Folly, though most of the press supported it.
  • Gold near Nome and oil at Prudhoe Bay later made Alaska far more valuable than its purchase price.
