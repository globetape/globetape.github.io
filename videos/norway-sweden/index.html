◀◀ DEEP DIVE · 2 MIN READ

Why Norway Became Richer Than Sweden

Norway is richer than Sweden today because of the sea off its coast and what it did with what was found there. In 1970 Sweden was the richer neighbour: $4,729 of GDP per person against Norway's $3,291, about 44% more. In 1965 the North Sea floor was divided along the median line, in late 1969 the giant Ekofisk field was found on Norway's side, and Norway taxed the oil heavily and saved the money abroad. By 2024 Norway's GDP per person was $89,889 and Sweden's $57,223.

Was Sweden really richer than Norway?

Yes. World Bank figures in current US dollars put Sweden's GDP per person at $4,729 in 1970 and Norway's at $3,291. Sweden had a large industrial base and was one of the richest countries in Europe. Norway was a smaller economy built on shipping, fishing and hydropower.

Nobody expected that to change. In 1958 the Geological Survey of Norway wrote to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs that the possibility of finding coal, oil or sulphur on the continental shelf off the Norwegian coast could be discounted.

How was the North Sea divided?

The discovery of the Groningen gas field in the Netherlands in 1959 changed minds about the North Sea. In May 1963 Norway proclaimed sovereignty over its continental shelf, and in 1965 it signed agreements with the United Kingdom and Denmark that drew the boundaries on the basis of the median line: a line halfway between the coasts.

That simple rule mattered enormously. It gave Norway a large share of the central and northern North Sea, including the area where Ekofisk would later be found.

What was Ekofisk?

Norway's first licensing round was announced on 13 April 1965. The first exploration well, drilled in the summer of 1966, was dry. Then, just before Christmas 1969, Phillips Petroleum told the Norwegian authorities it had found Ekofisk, which turned out to be one of the largest offshore oil fields ever discovered. Production started on 15 June 1971, and a series of major discoveries followed in the next few years.

Ekofisk lies in block 2/4, in the Norwegian sector, close to the median line with the United Kingdom.

How did Norway keep the money?

Norway made sure that a large share of the value went to the state. Oil companies pay the ordinary company tax plus a special petroleum tax, for a combined marginal rate of 78% on oil profits.

The surplus goes into the Government Pension Fund Global, often called the oil fund. It invests only abroad, so that the money does not overheat the Norwegian economy. At the end of September 2026 it was worth 22,014 billion kroner, around $2.3 trillion.

So was it luck or policy?

Both parts are true. The oil was under the seabed whether anyone looked or not, and the median line put the biggest early field on Norway's side. But the decisions about how to tax the oil and where to put the money were made by Norway, and they are why the windfall turned into lasting wealth.

Sweden is still one of the richest countries in the world. The difference is that, since the 1970s, Norway has had a second engine its neighbour does not have.
