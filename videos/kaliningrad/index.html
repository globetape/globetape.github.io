Kaliningrad: Why Does Russia Own It?

Kaliningrad Oblast · 23 November 2026 · 0:41

THE SHORT ANSWER

Why does Russia own Kaliningrad, a piece of land squeezed between Poland and Lithuania, inside the EU and NATO and two countries away from the rest of Russia? We rewind to 1945, when it was still the German city of Königsberg.

◀◀ DEEP DIVE · 2 MIN READ

How Kaliningrad Became a Piece of Russia Outside Russia

Russia owns Kaliningrad because the Soviet Union took over the area at the end of World War II, when it was the German city of Königsberg. When the USSR collapsed in 1991 and Lithuania became independent, Kaliningrad stayed with Russia but was left cut off from the rest of the country. Today it sits between Poland and Lithuania, both members of NATO and the EU.

Where is Kaliningrad?

Kaliningrad is a Russian exclave on the Baltic Sea, squeezed between Poland and Lithuania. It covers about 15,100 km² and is home to about one million people.

It does not touch the rest of Russia. To reach mainland Russia over land, you have to cross at least two countries: Lithuania and then Belarus or Latvia.

Was Kaliningrad always Russian?

No. Until 1945 it was German. The city was called Königsberg, and the surrounding region was the northern part of East Prussia.

At the end of World War II, the Potsdam Agreement of August 1, 1945 placed Königsberg and northern East Prussia under the Soviet Union. On July 4, 1946, the city was renamed Kaliningrad, after Mikhail Kalinin.

Why is Kaliningrad separated from Russia?

For decades the separation hardly mattered on the map, because the land between Kaliningrad and Russia was all part of the Soviet Union, Lithuania included.

That changed in 1991. The USSR recognised Lithuania's independence on September 6, 1991, and the Soviet Union itself dissolved in December of that year. Kaliningrad remained part of the new Russian Federation, but there was now an independent country between it and the rest of Russia. The exclave was cut off.

Is Kaliningrad surrounded by NATO and the EU?

Yes. Poland joined NATO in 1999, and Lithuania joined on March 29, 2004. Both countries joined the European Union on May 1, 2004. So by 2004, both of Kaliningrad's neighbours were in NATO and the EU.

Why does Russia keep Kaliningrad?

One key reason is its coastline. The port of Baltiysk, in the Kaliningrad region, is the base of Russia's Baltic Fleet. It is Russia's only port on the Baltic Sea that stays free of ice all year round, according to the US Naval Institute and DW.

That makes the exclave valuable even though it is physically separated from the rest of the country.

What is an exclave?

An exclave is a piece of a country that is cut off from its main territory by other countries. Kaliningrad fits that description exactly: it is Russian land, but the rest of Russia is two countries away.

The borders that created it were drawn in two steps. First, in 1945, the region passed from Germany to the Soviet Union. Then, in 1991, the Soviet Union broke apart, and the land between Kaliningrad and Russia became part of independent Lithuania.

Key takeaways

  • Kaliningrad was the German city of Königsberg until 1945.
  • The Potsdam Agreement placed it under the Soviet Union, and it was renamed Kaliningrad on July 4, 1946.
  • When the USSR collapsed in 1991 and Lithuania became independent, Kaliningrad was cut off from mainland Russia.
  • By 2004, its neighbours Poland and Lithuania were both in NATO and the EU.
  • Baltiysk is Russia's only Baltic port that never freezes, and the base of the Baltic Fleet.
