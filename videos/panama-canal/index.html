How the US Got the Panama Canal (and Gave It Back)

Panama Canal · 27 November 2026 · 8:21

EspañolPortuguêsFrançaisDeutsch

THE SHORT ANSWER

Panama Canal history starts with a surprising map: a 1903 treaty gave the United States sweeping powers over a strip across Panama. The Canal Zone ended in 1979; the waterway itself was handed over in 1999.

Rewind the map from Panama's separation from Colombia and France's abandoned works to the locks at Gatún and the first official crossing in 1914. Follow the Canal Zone through the Torrijos–Carter treaties and the 1999 handover, then see how the Panama Canal Authority runs the waterway today.

◀◀ DEEP DIVE · 6 MIN READ

How the US Got the Panama Canal and Gave It Back

The Panama Canal is a shortcut between oceans, but the oddest thing on its map used to be the strip of land beside it. For about 75 years the United States administered the Panama Canal Zone with powers it could exercise as if it were sovereign. The zone ended in 1979. Panama took full control of the waterway at noon on 31 December 1999. Those were two different handovers, and the difference is the key to reading the map.

Start at the Atlantic entrance

Imagine a ship entering from the Caribbean, on the Atlantic side. If you assume that its next ocean must lie west, look at the two entrances rather than the edges of the world map. Colón lies around 40 kilometres west of the Pacific end near Balboa. The route bends through the isthmus, but a ship crossing from Atlantic to Pacific travels generally southeast. The ocean names don't tell you which way to point the bow at this particular crossing.

The canal follows a corridor people were using long before anyone could sail through it. In 1513, Vasco Núñez de Balboa crossed the isthmus and became the first European documented to see the Pacific from the Americas. He called it the South Sea. Under Spanish rule, gold and silver from Peru crossed the isthmus by mule and river. The Camino de Cruces used the Chagres River as well as a trail over land. The Camino Real connected the Pacific side to Caribbean ports, including Portobelo. In 1534, Spain's king ordered a study of a canal route near the Chagres. The study did not turn into a canal, but the river remained central to the problem.

A railroad, then a French canal attempt

On 28 November 1821, Panama declared independence from Spain and joined the republic historians call Gran Colombia. After Gran Colombia broke up, the isthmus remained with New Granada, the predecessor of modern Colombia. That is why Panama's independence from Spain and its separation from Colombia are two different dates.

The California gold rush made the crossing useful to people travelling between the coasts of the United States. They could take a ship to the isthmus, cross it and board another ship on the Pacific side. In January 1855 a railroad built by a New York company completed its first ocean-to-ocean run. The line was about 77 kilometres long. Its two ends could now be reached from the other ocean without a ship having to go all the way around South America, but the passengers and cargo still had to change vehicles. A railway could not take the ship itself across.

Ferdinand de Lesseps, who had already built the Suez Canal, launched the French Panama project in 1880. The original plan was a sea-level passage without locks. In Panama the Chagres flooded, earth slid back into cuts and malaria and yellow fever devastated the workforce. By 1888 the French effort had switched to a plan with locks. The original company collapsed in February 1889. Death estimates for the French period vary, but the US Office of the Historian puts the toll at roughly 20,000. The abandoned works remained part of the next attempt.

Why Panama separated from Colombia in 1903

The USS Oregon demonstrated the problem in 1898. It was in San Francisco while the United States headed for war with Spain and needed the battleship in the Caribbean. To get there the ship went around South America, passing through the Strait of Magellan. The US naval account gives the voyage as more than 14,000 miles in 66 days. A canal was a strategic proposition as well as a commercial one.

Panama was not the only option. Many members of Congress favoured Nicaragua, where the lake and river would cover much of the route. Philippe Bunau-Varilla, a French engineer who backed Panama, sent senators a Nicaraguan stamp showing the Momotombo volcano in eruption. It was a striking image coming just after Mount Pelée destroyed Saint-Pierre on Martinique. In June 1902 the US Senate chose Panama by eight votes. Colombia's Senate rejected the proposed canal treaty the following year.

On 2 November 1903, the American gunboat USS Nashville arrived at Colón. Colombian soldiers came ashore the next morning. The railroad carried their generals across the isthmus without their troops. The generals were arrested and Panama declared its separation on 3 November. The troops at Colón left on 5 November; the United States recognised Panama on 6 November.

What the Canal Zone was

The treaty followed on 18 November 1903. Bunau-Varilla, a Frenchman representing Panama, signed before the other Panamanian delegates arrived in Washington. Article II granted the United States use, occupation and control of a zone ten miles wide in perpetuity. The cities of Panama and Colón were excluded. Article III gave the United States the powers it would have exercised if it were sovereign there. Those words matter: the map shows the extent of US administration, not a simple purchase of Panamanian territory.

Imagine the canal line drawn first, then a much wider strip on top of it. The waterway was only part of the zone. The area had its own governor, courts, police, schools and postage stamps. In 1970 the US census counted a little over 44,000 residents. The boundary was part of daily life even for people who never boarded a ship.

How the locks lift ships over the isthmus

The US took over the French works in May 1904. William Gorgas directed efforts to reduce mosquito-borne disease. The last case of yellow fever in Panama City was recorded in November 1905. In 1906 the US approved a lock canal. Rather than excavate the entire passage down to sea level, engineers dammed the Chagres at Gatún. The lake covered about 430 square kilometres and flooded several settlements, including Cruces on the old colonial route.

At the Atlantic end, a ship enters a chamber and the gates close behind it. Water flows in and the ship rises with the surface. Three chambers raise it about 26 metres to the level of Gatún Lake. Beyond the lake comes the Culebra Cut, almost 14 kilometres across the continental divide. At the other end, Pedro Miguel and Miraflores lower the ship back toward the Pacific. The shortcut takes ships up and over the isthmus, not straight through it at sea level.

Workers came from many places, including nearly 20,000 recruited from Barbados. The separate gold and silver payrolls reflected a system that also segregated housing and services. Hospital records from the US construction period counted 5,609 deaths. The SS Ancon made the first official crossing on 15 August 1914. A former French floating crane had already made a complete passage in January, before the official opening.

Why the return took twenty years

On 9 January 1964, a confrontation over flags at a high school in the Canal Zone escalated. More than twenty Panamanians and four US soldiers were killed. Panama broke off diplomatic relations the next day. In 1977, Omar Torrijos and Jimmy Carter signed two treaties in Washington. The US Senate approved each by 68 votes to 32, one vote more than the required two thirds. One treaty ended the zone and set out the transition; the other dealt with the canal's permanent neutrality and operation.

On 1 October 1979, the zone disappeared as an administrative entity. That did not immediately put the waterway under full Panamanian control. The transition continued for another twenty years, until noon on 31 December 1999. On a map these are separate events: first the strip disappears, then responsibility for operating the canal itself passes to Panama.

Who runs the canal now?

The Panama Canal Authority operates the waterway. In 2016 it opened new locks at Agua Clara and Cocolí for ships that could not fit the older chambers. Fresh water is still essential to the system. A passage through the original locks uses about 200 million litres, and in the 2023 drought the number of daily crossings was cut. In fiscal year 2026, the authority counted 13,951 vessel transits.

There are also commercial terminals near both ends. Operating one of those terminals is not the same thing as operating the canal. A 2026 Panamanian Supreme Court ruling struck down the concession covering two ports; the canal itself remains under the Panama Canal Authority. Look again at the ship off Colón: its direction makes the geography memorable, but knowing who runs the locks explains what the map means today.

Why did the United States control the Panama Canal Zone?
